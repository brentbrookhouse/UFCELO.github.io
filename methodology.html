Methodology

How the ratings on this site are calculated, and what they don't tell you

What this is

An Elo rating for every fighter in UFC history, computed from every fight on record, UFC 1 (1993) through the present. Elo is the same style of rating system used in chess: everyone starts equal, and a win against a strong opponent moves your number up more than a win against a weak one. It updates after every single fight, in chronological order.

This only covers UFC bouts. A fighter's record in other promotions (Bellator, ONE, PRIDE, regional shows, etc.) has no effect on their rating here — every fighter starts at the same number the day they debut in the UFC, regardless of how accomplished they were elsewhere.

How a rating changes after a fight

Every fighter starts at 1500. After a fight, the winner takes points from the loser in proportion to how surprising the result was — beating a much stronger opponent is worth far more than beating a much weaker one. A draw splits the movement evenly between both fighters.

Three things scale how much a single fight moves the needle:

  • Experience. Early fights swing a rating more than later ones — a fighter's first few UFC bouts move the number faster (both up and down) than fight #20 does, since there's less established history to weigh against.
  • Method of victory. A finish (KO/TKO/submission) counts for slightly more than a decision. Split and unanimous decisions are treated identically — judging in this sport is often close and inconsistent, and there's no reliable way to say a split decision was a "worse" or "less real" win than a unanimous one.
  • Title fights. A win in a real title fight — champion vs. challenger, or two title-holders unifying belts — is weighted higher than a normal fight. A fight billed as a title fight but not actually contested for the belt (for example, a challenger missing weight) doesn't get this bonus, even if the official result stands.

Round-of-finish is deliberately not factored in — a first-round knockout doesn't move the needle more than a hard-fought finish in a later round. A fighter grinding out a finish through sustained pressure can be just as impressive as a fast one; there's no reliable way to say one is always "more dominant" than the other from the round number alone.

Divisions are separate

A fighter's rating at Lightweight and their rating at Welterweight are two completely independent numbers, each built only from that fighter's fights at that specific weight. A fighter who's fought in three divisions has three separate ratings, not one blended number.

How a catchweight fight is ranked

A catchweight fight (a bout contracted at a weight that doesn't match a standard division limit) is placed into the smallest standard division whose weight limit is at or above the contracted weight. For example, a fight contracted at 158 lb falls above Lightweight's 155 lb limit, so it's counted as a Welterweight fight; a fight at 137 lb falls above Bantamweight's 135 lb limit, so it's counted as Featherweight.

Women's catchweight fights are bucketed the same way, but against the women's division limits (Strawweight, Flyweight, Bantamweight, Featherweight) rather than the men's — a catchweight bout between two women is never placed into a men's division regardless of the number on the scale.

Every catchweight result on this site is labeled as such, right next to the fight itself — in a fighter's fight log, on an event card, and in the Impact list. If a fighter's rating in a division looks lower than expected, check whether their recent fights there were actually catchweight bouts rather than fights at that division's real limit — a division built from only a couple of catchweight results doesn't carry the same weight as one built from a full career there.

The three views

All-Time

Full career ratings, uncapped, going back to a fighter's UFC debut. This is the primary view for "who's had the best career at this weight."

Current

Top 15 per division among fighters who've fought at least once in the trailing 24 months, with the reigning champion (where known) pinned above the list. Two years of inactivity is enough for a fighter to drop off this view entirely — that includes fighters who've retired or been released, since there's no separate "still on the roster" check; they simply age out on their own schedule.

Upcoming

The next three scheduled UFC events, with each fighter's current division rank shown next to their name — a snapshot of where they stand going in, not a prediction of who wins. A fighter moving up or down in weight for a specific fight is labeled as a debut at that new weight, separate from a fighter with no UFC record at all.

Overall / Pound-for-Pound mode

A toggle available on every view combines every division into one shared ladder, so a multi-division fighter's entire UFC career (all divisions combined) becomes one continuous number instead of several separate ones.

This is a genuinely useful lens, but it's the least rigorous number on the site. Fighters in different divisions essentially never fight each other, so there's no direct way to calibrate a flyweight's rating against a heavyweight's — the two pools only connect at all through champions who moved up or down in weight, or rare champion-vs-champion superfights. Treat the division-specific numbers as the solid ground and the Overall ladder as a rougher, complementary view.

A few things this site deliberately doesn't do

  • It doesn't use Glicko or any system with a built-in "confidence" score. That was evaluated and set aside — the sport's low fight counts per fighter make that kind of system behave unreliably compared to plain Elo, at least with the calibration tested so far.
  • It doesn't apply any bonus or penalty based on how a fight was judged, beyond the flat finish/decision split above.
  • It doesn't give a vacant-title fight the title-fight bonus. Winning a vacant belt is still a real, counted win — it just isn't weighted the same as beating a fighter who was already the champion.
